# CraftArc website: design brief for everything below the hero

Draft for alignment with Dash, 24 Nov 2026. The hero is already rebuilt to Dash's direction (issue 011): the approved question, answer and time line on the left, the existing video on the right as a placeholder, and no button in the body because the header carries Book a demo. This brief covers the rest of the homepage and the shared patterns every other page will reuse. It does not change any approved words. The CraftArc Website Guidance still decides the order of the content and the copy. This brief decides how that content looks and reads.

The brief went through two passes. Version 1 came from the research and an honest look at issue 010. A critique of version 1 against Dash's feedback and the evidence produced version 2, which is the proposal. Both are recorded below so the reasoning is visible.

## 1. What went wrong in issue 010

Dash's verdict was that the site would not score 2 out of 10 and was unreadable. Looking at the screenshots with that verdict in mind, the causes are clear, and most are design choices rather than copy.

The first and largest cause is scaffolding shown as content. The homepage carries twelve orange, all-caps, letter-spaced badges such as "DRAFT PLACEHOLDER, UNTIL CRAFTARC APPROVED COPY ARRIVES, SECTION 3", plus dashed orange rules beside each draft. They sit above the headlines, so the eye lands on them first. A reader cannot tell the message from the project management. The badges did their job for the launch check, but they made the page unreadable for anyone judging the design.

The second cause is that everything is a box. Claims, modules, steps, industries, onboarding steps and FAQ answers are all white cards with borders, shadows and gradient top bars. When every element is a card, nothing stands out, and the page reads as a grid of equal-weight tiles rather than a story. The same is true of the icon tiles: about forty blue gradient squares with white line icons, none of which carry information.

The third cause is the type system. There are too many sizes and weights, and too much text set in tracked capitals: orange section kickers, grey module labels, orange badges and status chips. Tracked capitals slow reading, and NN/g's guidance reserves them for glancing at single words, not for labels people must read. Headlines are set at weight 800 with tight letter spacing, which looks heavy when three of them stack in one screen. Card text drops to 15 or 16 pixels in a mid grey, which is legible but tiring.

The fourth cause is sameness of rhythm. Twelve sections, each with a kicker, a heavy headline and a grid, alternate between white, pale blue, pale grey, a navy band and a blue gradient with no logic a reader can feel. Nothing signals "this is the important part".

The fifth cause is that the page tells and never shows. The strongest proof CraftArc has is the product itself: real screens of a self-evaluation, a feedback coverage map, a drafted memo for the Executive Committee. On the homepage there is one screen, low in the proof section. The rest is text and decorative icons.

The sixth cause is repetition. "All without blocking days of leadership time" appears three times on the homepage, and the claims restate the hero. Repetition is normal in conversion copy, but here it lands in places where the reader expects new information.

## 2. What the research says

The evidence base for website conversion is mostly practitioner and vendor research, so the brief uses it for direction, not for promises. Where sources disagree, the brief follows the more conservative reading.

Clarity in the first screen matters most. Practitioner guides on B2B homepages agree that the hero must make clear, within a few seconds, what the product does, for whom and what outcome it gives, and that a clever headline that leaves the visitor unsure what is being sold costs conversions ([Twine](https://www.twine.net/blog/b2b-saas-landing-page-design/), [Tenspeed](https://www.tenspeed.io/blog/transform-your-homepage)). The approved hero already does this, and the new layout gives it room.

People scan before they read. NN/g's eye-tracking work found the F-shaped pattern: a horizontal read across the top, then a vertical scan down the left edge, and it says the F is the default when the design gives no strong cues ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/F-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/)). The design response is left-aligned headings that carry the story on their own, front-loaded words, and short blocks of text.

Readable text has known limits. Comfortable line length is about 50 to 75 characters ([Oregon State summary](https://blogs.oregonstate.edu/calverta/?p=1084), [Smashing Magazine](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2014/09/29/balancing-line-length-font-size-responsive-web-design/)). Body text needs a contrast ratio of at least 4.5 to 1 with its background, and text under 24 pixels counts as normal text for that rule ([W3C, WCAG 1.4.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/contrast-minimum), [WebAIM](https://webaim.org/articles/contrast/)). WebAIM notes that combinations at the minimum are still not very readable, so the brief targets far more than the minimum for body copy.

Proof belongs next to the decision. Analyses of high-converting demo pages report that most show customer logos and many show testimonials, and that proof placed near the form outperforms proof in the footer ([RevenueHero](https://revenuehero.io/2025-inbound-benchmark), [Chili Piper](https://www.chilipiper.com/resources/blog/boost-b2b-website-conversions)). CraftArc cannot show logos or a quote yet, so the brief uses what is real and allowed: product screens, the first-paying-client line and the founder's background.

"Book a demo" is the right wording. Vendor analyses of demo pages find "Book" or "Get" a demo outperforms "Request" a demo ([RevenueHero](https://www.revenuehero.io/blog/what-we-learned-from-1-000-b2b-website-cta-tests)). The site already uses "Book a demo" everywhere.

Video helps attention more than it helps conversion. Wistia reports up to 50 percent more engagement for video above the fold ([Wistia](https://wistia.com/blog/how-to-use-video-on-landing-pages)), but Unbounce's analysis of about 35,000 landing pages found video had little effect on conversion overall ([Unbounce](https://unbounce.com/landing-page-optimization/video-on-landing-pages)). The page must convert for someone who never presses play, so the video supports the hero but the copy and the screens carry the argument.

Length should match the decision. For high-consideration B2B purchases, longer pages that answer every question tend to do at least as well as short ones, provided they stay scannable ([CXL](https://cxl.com/long-form-or-short-form/)). A CEO deciding how to develop the leadership bench is a high-consideration buyer, so the brief keeps all the guidance's content but makes it faster to scan.

Section order on strong SaaS homepages tends to run hero, proof, outcomes, product demonstration, testimonials, pricing or a pricing teaser, FAQ and a closing call to action ([Krishaweb](https://www.krishaweb.com/blog/saas-homepage-conversion-best-practices/)). The guidance's order is close to this. The one difference worth noting is that proof sits seventh in the guidance, and the research would move it up. The brief does not reorder the guidance, but it adds a thin proof line directly under the hero.

## 3. Version 1 of the proposal

Version 1 kept all twelve sections as separate bands and fixed the type: lighter headline weight, 18-pixel body text, darker greys, no tracked capitals. It replaced the draft badges with a smaller grey pill. It kept the cards but removed their shadows, and it kept the icon tiles at a smaller size. It added product screens to the how-it-works section and moved the navy statement band to a pale tint.

## 4. Critique of version 1

Version 1 fixed legibility but not readability. It would still give Dash twelve equal bands, a wall of cards and forty icons, only quieter. It treated the badges as a style problem when they are a mode problem: the public page and the review page have different readers and need different views. It added screens to how-it-works but left the solution section as text in boxes, which is where a CEO most needs to see the product. It did not reduce the repetition of the time line. It also kept a button at the bottom of the page without asking whether Dash's "no Book a demo in the body" applies to the whole page or only to the hero. Version 2 answers each of these points.

## 5. Version 2: the proposal

### 5.1 Principles

One idea per screen, one visual per idea. Each band says one thing in its headline and proves it with one visual: a product screen, a diagram or a short list. A reader who reads only the headlines should get the whole story.

Show, then tell. Wherever the copy describes the product, a real screen sits beside it.

Quiet by default. Nothing on the page competes with the headline of its section. Colour, capitals, borders and shadows are used rarely and for a reason.

The public page is clean, and drafts live in review mode. The page renders without badges for a normal visitor. Adding `?review` to any address shows every draft badge, the "Not yet live" tag wording and every placeholder, exactly as they are now. `data-placeholder` stays in the markup, so the launch check still blocks launch until every draft is approved. This changes CLAUDE.md item 4, which today requires a visible badge, so it is Dash's call (decision 1). The Cockpit's "Not yet live" tag stays visible in both modes, because it is a statement of fact to the reader, not a draft marker.

### 5.2 Type, colour and spacing

Type is Inter throughout. Headlines use weight 700, not 800, with normal letter spacing. Section headlines run 36 to 40 pixels on desktop and 28 to 30 on a phone. The hero question stays the largest text on the page. Body text is 18 pixels with a line height of 1.65, set in near-black slate (#1e293b, about 14 to 1 on white). Secondary text uses #475569 (about 7.5 to 1) and only for captions and small print. Every text block is capped at 68 characters per line. Tracked capitals are limited to one short eyebrow per section, three words at most. Sentence case everywhere else, including buttons and labels.

Colour is white with one pale tint (#f8fafc) for alternate bands. Blue is the only action colour and appears on links and the header button. Orange appears only as the "Arc" accent in the logo and in the one diagram line that represents a leader's arc. The navy band goes, because a full-width dark slab in the middle of the page reads as the end of the page.

Spacing is generous and regular: 120 pixels between bands on desktop, 72 on a phone, and the same internal spacing in every band. Text is left-aligned in every band except the closing call to action.

### 5.3 Components

Cards are kept only for things that are genuinely parallel and comparable: the three modules and the six industries. Everything else is an open layout with hairline dividers. The gradient icon tiles go, apart from a small set in the solution diagram, where each icon names a module. Product screens get one consistent frame (a light browser bar, no heavy shadow) and a one-line caption that says what the reader is looking at and that the data is sample data.

### 5.4 The page, band by band

The guidance's twelve content modules stay in the same order with the same words. Visually they group into eight bands, so the page has a clear rhythm.

1. **Hero** (done). Question, answer, time line on the left. Video on the right. Directly beneath, a single thin proof line in small type: the approved stealth line and the founder's background (McKinsey, Microsoft and Siemens), so proof appears in the first screen as the research recommends.
2. **What you get** (three claims). Three columns on white, no boxes. Each column: the claim name as a small eyebrow, the claim in bold at 22 pixels, three short outcomes as a plain list, and "Delivered by" as a quiet text link to the module. The third claim's headline repeats the hero's time line, so the design gives that column a different visual, a simple "about 25 minutes per conversation" figure, to add information rather than repeat it.
3. **Why** (the problem and the method together). One pale band. The problem statement at 36 pixels, left-aligned, with "Most companies cannot answer either question." on its own line. Below a divider, the method lines as the answer. One band, two beats: the gap, then the way through.
4. **The product** (the solution). A single clean diagram on the left: the Leadership Cockpit on top with its "Not yet live" tag, the Review Cycle and the Development Arc below, the agent as a base line under all three. On the right, the three modules as short entries, each with who uses it and what the agent does. One real screen under the Review Cycle entry. This is where a CEO decides whether CraftArc is real, so it gets the most care.
5. **How it works** (the eight steps). The steps as a compact numbered list on the left. On the right, a product screen that changes as the reader scrolls through the steps (self-evaluation, coverage, the 360-degree dialogue list, the memo, progress), with the video available as a link rather than a second player. On a phone, each step shows its screen inline.
6. **Proof**. The approved stealth line as the headline. Beside it, the reserved slot for one result or one quote (shown only in review mode until it is approved), and a short founder note with a photo once Dash supplies one.
7. **Industries, how you start and who it is for**. One band with three parts in a calm sequence: six compact industry tiles with their status (live, coming next, coming soon), the four onboarding steps as a horizontal timeline, and the qualifier. The qualifier becomes two short lists, "CraftArc fits when" and "Wait if", ending with the approved line "If that commitment is not there yet, we will say so."
8. **FAQ and the close**. The seven questions as a single-column accordion, 760 pixels wide. Then the closing band, centred, with "Bring the question your board keeps asking." and one Book a demo button. This is the only button in the body (decision 2).

### 5.5 The Book a demo page

A two-column page: the short form on the right, and on the left the one line of copy, three short reasons to book (what the demo shows, how long it takes, who should come) and the proof line. The research on demo pages favours letting the visitor pick a time straight after the form, which turns a request into a booked meeting. That needs a scheduling tool such as HubSpot Meetings, Calendly or Chili Piper (decision 3).

### 5.6 Industry pages and About

Industry pages reuse bands 1 to 6 with the industry's own roles, as the guidance requires. Until each industry has its own screens and proof, the industry pages show the same product screens with a caption saying the sample data is from private equity. The About page becomes a short, warm page: the elevator pitch, the founder note with a photo, and the proof line.

### 5.7 How we will know it worked

Every change gets the existing checklist, plus five new automated checks added to the browser tests: every text element meets at least 4.5 to 1 contrast, body text is at least 17 pixels, no text block exceeds 75 characters per line, no section has more than one tracked-capital label, and the public view shows no draft badges while `?review` shows all of them. Then a fresh CEO reader test on the rebuilt page, and screenshots at 1440 and 390 pixels for Dash.

## 6. Decisions for Dash

1. **Drafts in review mode.** Public pages show no draft badges; `?review` shows them; the launch check still blocks launch. This replaces CLAUDE.md item 4's visible-badge rule.
2. **Buttons in the body.** The header button plus one closing Book a demo band at the bottom of the page, and no other buttons. Or the header button alone.
3. **Scheduling.** Add a calendar to the Book a demo page after the form, with a tool Dash chooses.
4. **Eight bands.** Group the guidance's twelve modules into eight visual bands, keeping the order and the words.

## 7. Dash's decisions (24 Nov 2026)

1. Drafts in review mode: yes. 2. Buttons: every Book a demo button lives in the header, none in the body. 3. Scheduling: coming soon, kept on the launch to-do list. 4. Eight bands: yes.

## 8. Version 3: the conversion pass

Dash asked for one more pass whose single test is whether the reader wants to reach for Book a demo. Decision 2 changes the mechanics of that. With no buttons in the body, the page cannot ask for the meeting section by section, so it has to build the wish to act and then make the one button impossible to miss when the wish peaks. Version 3 does that in five ways.

**The header button is the only door, so it is always open and always visible.** The header stays fixed on every page and every width, the button keeps the same position and wording, and on industry pages it carries the industry to the form. Nothing else in the header is blue. At three moments where conversion research says intent peaks (after the reader has seen the product, after the proof, and at the close) the button gives one soft, single pulse as the band comes into view. It never loops, and it does not move at all for readers who ask for reduced motion.

**Every band ends higher than it starts.** Each band opens with its claim and closes with the strongest specific it has: a sourced number (about 25 minutes per conversation), a real screen, the first-paying-client line or the founder's background. The reader should finish each band slightly more certain than they began it, which is the Hormozi test of likelihood of achievement.

**Proof arrives in the first screen.** A thin line under the hero copy states the two strongest facts the site may use: the first paying client is a private equity firm, and the founder's background at McKinsey, Microsoft and Siemens. Logos and quotes would be stronger, and they replace this line when the client consents.

**The product is shown, not described.** In How it works, the eight steps sit on the left and a framed product screen sits on the right, changing as the reader moves through the steps. This is the moment a CEO decides CraftArc is real. On a phone, each step carries its own screen inline.

**Anxiety is removed before the end.** The FAQ answers the seven questions CEOs asked, the qualifier tells the reader when CraftArc is not right for them (the approved "If that commitment is not there yet, we will say so" is a form of risk reversal), and the close restates the board's question so the last thing the reader sees is the reason to book. The Book a demo page then lists what the demo will show, so the click has a clear payoff, and keeps the form to five visible fields.

The visual system from version 2 stands, with one change: the hero, industries and close use the same soft blue-to-white wash as the header, so the page opens and ends in the same colour and the middle bands sit on white and one pale tint.
